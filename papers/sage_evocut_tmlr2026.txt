Abstract
Integer programming (IP) is central to many combinatorial optimization tasks but remains
challenging due to its NP-hard nature. A practical way to improve IP solvers is to manually
design inequality families that exploit problem structure. However, this creative process
requires deep expertise and has therefore been diﬃcult to automate. Our proposed frame-
work, Symbolic Acceleration via Generative Evolution ( SAGE), automates symbolic dis-
covery of acceleration inequality families at the modeling level. It reasons over a symbolic
IP formulation and a natural language description of the problem to discover a reusable
set of acceleration inequality families that can be instantiated for each problem instance.
These acceleration inequality families are selected through empirical admissibility and im-
pact screening rather than mathematical proofs. SAGE (i) initializes a population of candi-
date acceleration inequality families via an initializer agent that uses an LLM; (ii) it screens
candidates for admissibility on a small development admissibility set for solution feasibility
and the separation of the relaxation solution; and (iii) it iteratively reﬁnes the population
of inequalities through evolutionary crossover and mutation agents. Across seven MILP
benchmarks, SAGE reduces optimality gaps by up to 62.1% relative to baseline MILP
formulations solved with a ﬁxed time budget and reaches target gaps up to 5.51× faster
according to shifted geometric mean speedup. Comprehensive ablations show its robustness
across diﬀerent LLM backends and across solvers and internal cut settings. Code

In this study, we focus on static model augmentation before calling the solver for Mixed Integer Linear
Programming (MILP). Starting from a base MILP formulation, we generate additional linear inequality
families, called acceleration inequality families, intended to improve solver eﬃciency. Many widely studied
problem classes admit problem-speciﬁc inequalities derived in the OR literature. However, we aim for a
general pipeline that operates directly on a symbolic MILP formulation shared across instances. We refer to
this shared formulation as the symbolic MILP formulation and to its specialization with instance data as the
instantiated MILP. Our proposed method, Symbolic Acceleration via Generative Evolution ( SAGE), uses
LLM agents guided by evolutionary search to discover and reﬁne such families by reasoning over a symbolic
MILP formulation and a natural language description of the problem. In particular, SAGE learns a reusable
set of acceleration inequality families during development and instantiates them for each problem instance
at deployment time (Fig. 1). SAGE is a general framework for symbolic discovery at the modeling level and
integrates into standard solver pipelines across diﬀerent MILP models.

Together, this paper makes three contributions. First, we introduce SAGE, a general framework for symbolic
discovery of empirically screened acceleration inequality families at the modeling level . Given a symbolic
MILP formulation and a small admissibility set, SAGE discovers and selects a reusable set of acceleration
inequality families that can be instantiated for each problem instance. Second, we evaluate SAGE on seven
diverse MILP problems and show consistent solver speedups on unseen instances. The largest row-average
checkpoint gap reduction is 62.1%, and the largest row-average target-gap speedup is 5.51×. On the four
benchmarks with published valid inequalities, the discovered families match or exceed the mean speedups
from those inequalities under the same protocol (Section 5.5). Third, we provide an ablation and robustness
analysis of SAGE: (a) data eﬃciency by varying the sizes of the impact-assessment and admissibility sets
(Section 5.8), (b) LLM sensitivity across DeepSeek-R1, Gemini 3 Pro, and GPT-5.1 (Table 2), (c) solver
robustness by transferring the same discovered acceleration inequality families across solvers (Section 5.7.1),
and (d) interaction with solver internal cut generation by comparing Gurobi with internal cuts disabled vs.
default settings (Section 5.7.2).
2 Related Work
Recent work has explored using LLMs to automate various stages of OR workﬂows, from problem under-
standing to algorithm generation Liu et al. (2026); Huang et al. (2024). A central line of research focuses
on converting natural language descriptions into optimization formulations, initiated by the NL4Opt com-
petition Ramamonjison et al. (2023) and advanced by systems such as OptiMUS AhmadiTeshnizi et al.
(2024), ORLM Huang et al. (2025), and LM4Opt Ahmed & Choudhury (2024). Recent frameworks with
multiple agents incorporate dedicated veriﬁcation agents to ensure correctness before generating code that

can be passed directly to a solver, including the chain of experts Xiao et al. (2023) and the staged agent
architecture of Mostajabdaveh et al. (2024). These eﬀorts focus on modeling ﬁdelity. In contrast, SAGE
targets eﬃciency after a base formulation already exists: it proposes additional acceleration inequality fam-
ilies, screens them empirically on held-out instances, and ranks them by measured eﬀect on time-limited
solver progress. To our knowledge, prior research has not studied the automatic discovery of reusable accel-
eration inequality families that augment the symbolic model before the solver is called. Another emerging
direction uses LLMs within evolutionary frameworks to generate heuristics for combinatorial optimization.
These methods aim to boost heuristic algorithm performance by modifying parts of the algorithm code (e.g.,
heuristic rules) Liu et al. (2024); Ye et al. (2024). FunSearch Romera-Paredes et al. (2024) couples a frozen
LLM with evolutionary search and an evaluator to discover heuristics, while the evolution of heuristics Liu
et al. (2024) reﬁnes natural language "thoughts" and executable code using evolutionary strategies. ReEvo Ye
et al. (2024) introduces reﬂective evolution with pairwise comparisons and reﬂections over longer horizons
to improve code generated by LLMs iteratively. While these works evolve heuristics or code, SAGE evolves
reusable acceleration inequality families. More broadly, machine learning for MILP solving has targeted
decisions inside branch-and-bound: learned branching approximates strong branching or variable-selection
policies Alvarez et al. (2017); Khalil et al. (2016); Gasse et al. (2019); learned cut selection chooses which
generated cuts to apply Tang et al. (2020); Turner et al. (2023); Paulus et al. (2022); Wang et al. (2023);
separator-conﬁguration methods such as L2Sep, cold-start LLM conﬁguration, and dynamic separator con-
ﬁguration decide which solver separators to activate during the solve Li et al. (2023); Lawless et al. (2025);
Ye et al. (2026); learned or optimized cut-generation mechanisms select cut-generating functions Chételat &
Lodi (2023); Cheng & Basu (2024); and learned primal heuristics aim to ﬁnd incumbents early Shen et al.
(2021). The central distinction is that these methods modify runtime policies within the solver’s branch-
and-bound process, whereas SAGE operates one level earlier: it augments the symbolic MILP formulation
before the solver is called, producing reusable acceleration inequality families that can then be solved with
any branching, cut-selection, separator-conﬁguration, cut-generation, or primal-heuristic policy. Thus our
contribution is not a replacement for ML solver policies but an orthogonal modeling-level layer; the same
learned runtime policy can be run on the baseline formulation or on the SAGE-augmented formulation.
3 Preliminaries
To motivate an automatic yet reliable generator of acceleration inequality families, we ﬁrst recall the baseline
MILP and deﬁne the acceleration inequality families that SAGE discovers. We then relate them to the linear
relaxation and to the classical notions of valid and optimality-preserving inequalities. Our goal is to discover
useful acceleration inequality families, including valid inequalities when possible, and to retain them through
empirical screening and measured solver progress.
We consider an MILP of the form
max
{
c⊤x + h⊤y : Ax + Gy ≤ b, x ∈ Zn ≥0, y ∈ Rp ≥0 } , (1)
with integer variables x∈ Zn ≥0 and continuous variables y∈ Rp ≥0. Its feasible set is
S = { (x, y)∈ Zn ≥0× Rp ≥0 : Ax + Gy≤ b}.
Throughout the paper we use the term additional inequality family to mean a set of linear inequalities
appended to the MILP. We write such a family in the form
C :=

where R is an index set and z denotes auxiliary variables introduced by the added inequalities. Including
z allows the inequality family to encode additional logical relations. In practice, one may add inequality
families only to speed up solving. We call any such added inequality family, tailored to a particular problem
class, an acceleration inequality family.

Deﬁnition 3.1 (Acceleration inequality family) . An additional linear inequality family added to an
MILP with the explicit aim of reducing solution time. Such a family need not be composed only
of proven valid inequalities or proven optimality-preserving inequalities; in this work, it is retained
only after empirical admissibility checks that (i) stored reference solutions remain feasible on a small
admissibility set and (ii) at least one stored LP-relaxation solution is separated, with quality assessed
by solver progress on an impact-assessment set.
Relaxing integrality yields the linear programming (LP) relaxation
max
{
c⊤x + h⊤y : Ax + Gy ≤ b, x ∈ Rn ≥0, y ∈ Rp ≥0 } , (2)
whose feasible set (the linear relaxation set) is
P ={ (x, y)∈ Rn ≥0× Rp ≥0 : Ax + Gy≤ b}.
Although Eq. ( 2) can be solved in polynomial time, its optimum is generally fractional (i.e., not in S). This
motivates cutting-plane and branch-and-cut methods, in which a solver adds valid inequalities dynamically
during the solve. By contrast, SAGE produces acceleration inequality families at the user or model level
that are appended to the MILP before calling the solver.
Formally, an inequality
w⊤(x, y) ≤ δ
is valid for a set Q if and only if Q⊆{ (x, y) : w⊤(x, y)≤ δ}. Designing strong valid inequalities tailored to
a speciﬁc problem class typically requires nontrivial proofs and insight into the combinatorial structure of
the problem.
Separately, an inequality is optimality-preserving for Eq. ( 1) if it does not change the optimal value Z ∗ =
max{c⊤x + h⊤y : (x, y)∈ S}, i.e.,
max{c⊤x + h⊤y : (x, y)∈ S, w ⊤(x, y)≤ δ} = Z ∗,
even though it may remove other feasible solutions Laporte & Semet (1999); da Cunha et al. (2015). We
reserve the term optimality-preserving for inequalities that are proved to satisfy this. Appendix D reviews
the geometry of valid and optimality-preserving inequalities and their eﬀect on the linear relaxation set
(Appendix D.1), polyhedra and facets (Appendix D.2), and facet-deﬁning valid inequalities (Appendix D.3).
SAGE’s acceleration inequality families diﬀer from several classical objects. A valid inequality is proved
to contain all feasible integer points in S. A formulation strengthening is a reformulation or augmentation
with established validity for the model class. Benders cuts are derived from a decomposition subproblem
and added through a master-subproblem scheme. By contrast, a SAGE acceleration inequality family is
proposed before solving and kept because it passes empirical optimal solution screening (OSS) and LP-
separation checks and improves solver progress. OSS checks whether the family preserves stored reference
solutions on sampled instances. No global proof of validity or optimality preservation is assumed.
Modern MILP solvers routinely inject cuts that are general purpose during solving and that apply to many
formulations ( Chvátal, 1973; Balas, 1979). Commercial solvers such as Gurobi enable these cut-generation
routines by default or through solver parameters ( Gurobi Optimization LLC , 2026). Separately, problem-
speciﬁc structure often gives rise to inequalities that improve solver speed in vehicle routing, network design,
scheduling, and graph models ( Toth & Vigo , 2002; Lysgaard et al. , 2004; Costa et al. , 2009; Queyranne
& Wang , 1991; Aref et al. , 2020). Given a symbolic MILP formulation, SAGE automatically proposes,
empirically screens, and evolves acceleration inequality families to improve solve time.
4 Proposed Method
We propose SAGE, an evolutionary algorithm powered by multiple LLM agents to iteratively generate and
reﬁne acceleration inequality families that speed up solving across instances of a given MILP model. Candi-
date acceptance is determined by empirical admissibility and impact screens, not by a proof of global validity.

in three main phases: (1) data preprocessing, (2) population initialization (driven by the initializer agent),

Inequality Admissibility Screen, and the Inequality Impact Scorer.
We begin by splitting available development instances for a given MILP into an impact-assessment set Dimp
and an admissibility set Dadm. We use Dadm only as a development screen for OSS and LP-separation
checks, and Dimp to score candidate family quality. For every instance i∈ Dimp, we run the baseline MILP
under a ﬁxed computational budget. We use Gurobi’s WorkLimit parameter, set to 10 ( Gurobi Optimization
LLC, 2026), and record its terminal optimality gap, gapref (i). We construct Dimp so that gapref (i) > 0 for
all i∈ Dimp. This reference gap serves as the baseline for assessing the eﬀect of candidate acceleration
inequality families on solver performance within SAGE. For the smaller subset Dadm, we also store (i) a
solver solution (ˆx∗ i , ˆy∗ i ) from solving the MILP to an optimality gap tolerance of 10−5 (for the OSS check)
and (ii) the LP relaxation optimum (xLP i , yLP i ) (for the LP-separation check). Thus, Dimp stores gapref (i),
while Dadm stores (ˆx∗ i , ˆy∗ i ), (xLP i , yLP i ), and gapref (i) for each instance i.

inequality families generated so far, and (3) instructions to propose novel acceleration inequality families
intended to separate poor LP-relaxation behavior in Eq. ( 2). The LLM response includes a brief idea for
the proposed family together with executable code (e.g., Pyomo Hart et al. (2011)). Each LLM-proposed

With the initial population of admissible and scored candidate families ready, the SAGE algorithm evolves
the population for T generations. The main steps of the evolution are as follows: (i) Elitism: The top re
fraction of families with the highest inequality impact scores carry over to the next generation. (ii) Selection:
Parent families are picked via tournament selection based on inequality impact score. (iii) Crossover: With
probability Pc, two parents are passed to a Crossover LLM, which attempts to produce a new child family
Co by merging parent features. (iv) Mutation: With probability Pm, a single parent undergoes a Mutation
LLM, which alters coeﬃcients or terms to produce a variant Cm. (v) Admissibility Screening & Impact
Scoring: Each newly created candidate (Co or Cm) is checked by the Inequality Admissibility Screen. Failed
candidates are discarded, and a feedback prompt is provided to the LLM (up to a predeﬁned maximum retry
limit). Admissible candidates are then forwarded to the Inequality Impact Scorer. Their inequality impact
scores are computed and then they are added to the next generation. At each iteration, reproduction (i.e.,
the generation of a new candidate family) continues until the next population reaches its required size. This
evolutionary process then repeats for T generations. In each reproduction step, SAGE chooses whether
to perform crossover (with probability Pc) or mutation (with probability Pm). Once the operation type is
chosen, one of the corresponding agents is selected uniformly at random.
SAGE draws on seven agents, three for mutation and four for crossover. Each mutation agent rewrites a
single parent family. General Mutation modiﬁes its structure or its coeﬃcients. Lifted Mutation strengthens
the parent in the style of lifting ( Balas, 1975; Desrochers & Laporte , 1991), by assigning coeﬃcients to
variables its inequalities leave out, or by adding auxiliary structure. Exploratory Mutation moves away
from the parent to reach a distinct family. Each crossover agent combines two parent families. Intersection
Crossover keeps useful structural elements from each parent. Complementary Crossover looks for structure
that neither parent covers. Hybrid Crossover takes structure from one parent and numerical or conditional
features from the other. Min Violation Crossover merges the parents so that the child keeps the recorded
reference solutions feasible, which is what the OSS check requires. Appendix B gives the full prompts.
5 Experiments and Results
We structure our evaluation around three questions. RQ1 asks whether SAGE improves solver eﬃciency on
unseen instances. We measure checkpoint optimality gaps and time to target gaps. RQ2 asks how sensitive
SAGE is to the sizes of the impact assessment set Dimp and admissibility set Dadm. We vary |Dimp| and
|Dadm| and report test set gap metrics for the selected acceleration inequality family. RQ3 asks how the
evolutionary search shapes the acceleration inequality families that the LLMs generate. We compare the
acceptance rates of the parent-based operators with those of the one-shot initializer and track inequality
impact scores across generations. We also compare SAGE with a token matched initializer only control on
JSSP.
The subsections answer these questions in order. Sections 5.2 and 5.3 answer RQ1 with checkpoint gaps
and time to target, and Section 5.4 then checks that the families measured there exclude no proven optimal
solution. Sections 5.5 to 5.7 test the RQ1 result against published valid inequalities, learned solver policies,
a second solver, and two internal cut settings. Section 5.8 answers RQ2, Section 5.9 answers RQ3, and
Section 5.10 interprets one selected family.

T = 20 generations with a population size of |P| = 8 , using a mutation probability of Pm = 0 .3, an elitism
ratio of re = 0.2, and a crossover probability of Pc = 0.7. Agents may retry candidate family generation up
to three times if admissibility checks fail. We cap one complete search at 20 hours, and every search ﬁnished
within this cap. The runtime varies with the LLM inference provider, its latency and rate limits, and the
solver time spent scoring candidates. We use Gemini 3 Pro ( Google DeepMind , 2025) as the main LLM in
SAGE (Table 22 in Appendix G gives the full API settings and approximate costs). Unless otherwise stated,
the main results use the family selected from the Gemini 3 Pro SAGE run for each benchmark. To assess
sensitivity to the LLM, we also run SAGE with DeepSeek-R1 Guo et al. (2025) and GPT-5.1 OpenAI (2025)
(Table 2).
Our four core benchmarks include: Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP), Multi-Commodity Network Design
(MCND), Capacitated Warehouse Location Problem (CWLP), and Job Shop Scheduling Problem (JSSP).
Additionally, we report results on the Pickup and Delivery Problem with Time Windows (PDPTW), In-
ternational Mathematical Olympiad 2025 Problem 6 (IMO6), and a unit commitment benchmark with a
horizon under one hour (SHUC) (Table 2). IMO6 is a newly formulated MILP benchmark of a 2025 IMO
problem, so LLMs are less likely to have been exposed to optimization literature speciﬁc to it. PDPTW
and SHUC are practically motivated models with many constraints, and some of our instances are included
in MIPLIB Koch et al. (2011). The test set sizes are 54 (TSP), 81 (MCND), 36 (CWLP), 80 (JSSP), 165
(PDPTW), 34 (IMO6), and 56 (SHUC). Appendix F provides a dedicated subsection for each benchmark
problem, including its formulation, instance source, and dataset details: TSP (Appendix F.1), MCND (Ap-
pendix F.2), CWLP (Appendix F.3), JSSP (Appendix F.4), PDPTW (Appendix F.5), IMO6 (Appendix F.6),
and SHUC (Appendix F.7). Tables 20 and 21 in Appendix F summarize the held-out test set scales and
the synthetic development source scales, respectively. During evolution, SAGE relies on relatively small
development datasets Dimp (default size 10) and Dadm (default size 2), as detailed in Section 4. The default
|Dadm| = 2 is used only for candidate screening during development. We do not use benchmark test instances
during development. For all problems except IMO6, both Dimp and Dadm are drawn from synthetic gener-

For IMO6, all instances are generated and split into Dimp, Dadm, and Dt.

5.4 Post-hoc Screening Against Proven Optimal Solutions
This check applies to the same selected families evaluated in Sections 5.2 and 5.3. SAGE screens candidate
acceleration inequality families using the small admissibility set Dadm during development (Section 4). In
the default setting, this admissibility set has only two instances. After selecting an acceleration inequality
family, we run an additional oﬄine check on the held-out test sets to answer the following question: Does the
selected SAGE acceleration inequality family ever exclude a proven optimal solution that we can otherwise
compute? This post-hoc check is not an extra step required by SAGE at deployment time. For each test
instance in every benchmark, we run the baseline MILP for 48 h and record the instances where the solver
proves optimality. Across the full test sets, proven optima are obtained for CWLP at the rate of 36/36.
Similarly, the rates are 58/80 for JSSP, 77/81 for MCND, 54/54 for TSP, 9/34 for IMO6, and 53/56 for
SHUC. For each such instance, we ﬁx the original decision variables to the proven optimal solution, append
the selected SAGE acceleration inequality family, and solve only for any auxiliary variables introduced by
the family to test feasibility. In all cases, the feasibility check succeeds. We ﬁnd no case in which the selected
SAGE acceleration inequality family excludes a proven optimal solution in our test set. For PDPTW, none
of the 48 h baseline runs proves optimality (0/165), so the above check was not possible. We therefore report
the following bound consistency results: using the 2000 s augmented run from Table 2, (i) the 2000 s lower
bound does not exceed the 48 h upper bound and (ii) the 48 h lower bound does not exceed the 2000 s upper
bound. This condition holds for PDPTW at the rate of 165/165. Across the instances for which this check
is possible, we detect zero exclusions of the proven optimum. This test set result provides empirical evidence
of optimality preservation for the selected families on the evaluated instances.
Appendix E.2 reports a related held-out LP separation check. For each test instance, we ﬁx the original
variables to the baseline LP optimum, append the selected acceleration inequality family, and solve only for
auxiliary variables. Table 12 in Appendix E.2 shows 100.0% LP optimum separation on TSP, CWLP, JSSP,
PDPTW, and IMO6, 88.9% on MCND, and 85.7% on SHUC. This test complements the preservation check
by measuring separation of one baseline LP optimum per test instance.

5.5 Comparison with Static Literature Augmentation
For the four benchmarks with well-known published valid inequalities, we also compare against adding these

5.7.1 Transfer Across Solvers
This cross-solver robustness ablation checks whether the observed gains are speciﬁc to a particular solver

MILP solvers, Gurobi and HiGHS. Each solver uses its default settings unless speciﬁed in Section 5.1, and
each run uses the same 2000 s time limit and seven checkpoints as Table 2. Table 5 reports ¯∆g under each
solver.
In Table 5, the HiGHS 2000 s means are positive for 7/7 benchmarks and range from 5.7% (MCND) to 64.2%

and smaller than Gurobi on the other 5 benchmarks.

This check shows how much of SAGE’s beneﬁt remains when the solver’s own cutting-plane machinery

disabled and under default internal cuts, using the same test sets, 2000 s limit, and seven checkpoints as
Table 2. Table 6 reports ¯∆g under each cut setting.

6 Discussion
Starting with RQ1, Table 2 and Appendix E.1 show that SAGE can reduce the optimality gap at successive
checkpoints under the same time budgets. The trajectory checks in Appendix E.9, Appendix E.10, and
Appendix E.11 show the same eﬀect for individual instances. Table 2 also indicates that diﬀerent LLMs
lead to diﬀerent families and levels of improvement. Therefore, choosing an LLM among the multiple LLM
options of SAGE matters. The main LLM result is that Gemini 3 Pro has been generally the strongest
backend in our experiments. This pattern justiﬁes our use of the Gemini 3 Pro acceleration inequality family
as the selected alternative in the rest of the experiments. Complementing this, Table 3 shows faster times
to reach target gaps across benchmarks. Table 17 in Appendix E.6 shows that the selected families tighten
the LP relaxation on nearly every test instance, so part of the deployment gain may come from a stronger
initial relaxation, while the amount of tightening and the added model size vary by benchmark. SAGE often
increases the number of instances that reach the target gap within the time limit. We have also observed on
rare occasions that SAGE acceleration inequality families may have negative impact. When a discovered
acceleration inequality family fails to improve the relaxed model for a particular instance, it increases model
size and overhead nonetheless, leading to a slight deterioration of the overall performance (Appendix E.12).
To test robustness beyond gap improvements, we reported on four complementary experiments. First, the
post-hoc screening against proven-optimal solutions and the held-out LP separation check in Section 5.4
indicate a reduced chance that the observed speedups come from excluding good integer solutions. Second,
18
=====PAGE 19
Published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research (10/2026)
the cross-solver ablation in Section 5.7.1 suggests that the same discovered families can transfer beyond
one solver. Third, the internal-cut ablation in Section 5.7.2 shows that SAGE remains useful when cut
generation inside Gurobi changes. Fourth, the learned-policy factorial in Section 5.6 evaluates the same
modeling claim against learned cut-selection and separator-conﬁguration policies. The learned-policy results
are consistent with complementarity between SAGE’s model-layer augmentation and the learned solver-layer
policies. Together, these results suggest that SAGE provides a static model augmentation at deployment after
a separate discovery stage. This augmentation is separate from solver cut policies and learned runtime policies
because it changes the formulation before the solver is called, while those policies act during solving. We also
observe that the magnitude of improvement depends on the interaction between the compact formulation,
the selected acceleration inequality family, and the solver. Mature benchmarks can still show large gains
when the compact baseline leaves useful model structure. Finally, we focus on improving a given symbolic
formulation without callbacks or domain-speciﬁc separation code. Accordingly, our baselines use standard
compact formulations (Appendix F) rather than a tailored branch-and-bound method for each benchmark
problem.
The discovery and payback analysis in Section 5.3 separates the search cost from repeated deployment. The
discovery stage carries the cost that is paid once per problem class, while the added cost at each deployment
is the time to instantiate the family for one instance. SAGE needs no callback, no separation routine, and
no change to the solver, so the augmented model enters a standard solver pipeline. The results show that
the search cost can be amortized across repeated deployments, but the required number varies by problem
class. The same selected families also hold across the solvers in Section 5.7.1 and the internal cut settings in
Section 5.7.2, so one search covers more than one deployment conﬁguration. The tested distribution, solver,
hardware, target gap, time limit, and search budget determine the estimate of payback in compute time.
API prices determine the separate monetary search cost.
The method sensitivity check in Section 5.7.3 shows that the selected family improves solver progress under
both methods, with the magnitude of the gain depending on the method.
The static literature augmentation comparison in Section 5.5 and Appendix E.7 clariﬁes how SAGE’s model-
layer gains compare with existing expert valid inequalities. Valid inequalities can be strong under the same
static augmentation protocol, as shown in Table 18 in Appendix E.7. The SAGE acceleration inequality
families, however, yield even larger mean speedups than the instantiated static literature valid inequalities on
CWLP, JSSP, and TSP. On MCND both methods are essentially equivalent. Taken together, our analysis
suggests that SAGE performs symbolic search for useful acceleration inequality families at the modeling
level. The selected SAGE families include both familiar forms of inequalities and more specialized algebraic
forms. This search can recover high-value structure that expert formulations of classic problems also exploit,
including cut-set capacity, assignment and opening links, cover logic, ordering constraints, and workload
bounds. For TSP, the smaller margin is consistent with the literature baseline already adding lifted MTZ
ordering rows that overlap with the selected SAGE family. For the lesser explored benchmarks, such as IMO6
and SHUC, the same search can produce more specialized families tied to the symbolic formulation. Thus,
SAGE is a symbolic search procedure whose empirical screening determines which acceleration inequality
family is useful for the given compact formulation.
The anonymized RNTD control in Appendix E.5 makes direct recall of a published formulation or its speciﬁc
inequalities less plausible. Together, RNTD and IMO6 distinguish formulation speciﬁc exposure from general
optimization knowledge. Their unpublished or newly formulated MILPs reduce cues for direct recall while
leaving general optimization knowledge available to the LLM.
Turning to RQ2, Table 7 suggests that small admissibility sets may suﬃce, while larger impact-assessment
sets generally yield stronger families. The default admissibility set is the smallest one we tested, and on
JSSP it still yields a family that performs close to the largest setting, so a small admissibility set is enough
to produce a strong family. SAGE derives acceleration inequality families from underlying problem logic
rather than patterns speciﬁc to a dataset. In our experiments, these families remain eﬀective even when the
test distribution or size diﬀers from those used for admissibility screening and impact assessment. This is
consistent with our choice of split: Dimp and Dadm are synthetic, while Dt is a held-out public benchmark
set. As a practical consideration, the sizes of Dimp and Dadm create a tradeoﬀ between runtime and quality.

Larger impact-assessment sets tend to improve deployment performance. Larger admissibility sets can also
help, but to a lesser extent. Both increase SAGE development runtime because each candidate family is

For RQ3, the paired JSSP experiment in Section 5.9, Table 9, shows that evolutionary search improves on the

7 Conclusion and Future Direction
SAGE automates acceleration inequality family generation, a step that augments a MILP model before
solving and is typically driven by experts. Across seven MILP benchmarks, SAGE discovers reusable and
interpretable families, ranging from expert strengthening patterns on mature benchmarks to more specialized
families on less standard models. These families can be appended to MILPs in standard solver pipelines and
often improve solver progress under time limits. The search runs once per problem class, and Section 5.3 re-
ports its cost and the number of deployment solves that recover it. We expect the approach to be particularly
useful for custom or newly formulated MILPs, where established formulation ideas may be limited.
Two design choices deﬁne the present scope of SAGE. Empirical screening evaluates solution preservation
and LP separation on sampled instances, while formal global guarantees require a separate proof. The current
method instantiates acceleration inequality families before solving. Runtime separation and dynamic addition
remain future extensions.
This scope motivates two directions for future work: (1) The current screening relies on recorded solutions on
a small admissibility set. Integrating automated formal proof tooling could provide theoretical guarantees
(e.g., discovering acceleration inequality families composed of provably valid inequalities or families that
provably preserve optimality) Yang et al. (2025). (2) SAGE currently inserts instantiated acceleration
inequality families into the MILP model before the solution process begins. Dynamically separating and
adding inequalities via callbacks during the solve could further improve solver performance, but requires
generating an eﬃcient separation algorithm for each discovered family.
Broader Impact Statement
SAGE is a study of acceleration inequality families for MILP solving. It uses benchmark and synthetic MILP
instances and no human subject data. By improving solver progress under ﬁxed budgets, the method may
reduce compute time and energy use, but it may also improve the performance of downstream optimization
systems in high-stakes domains such as energy, logistics, scheduling, and public-sector resource allocation.
The main technical risk is that empirically screened acceleration inequality families are not guaranteed to be
globally valid or optimality preserving on all unseen instances. Users should validate any generated family
in the target domain, keep a baseline fallback, monitor for regressions, and not treat empirical screening as
proof of validity. Releasing reproducible evaluation code and synthetic generators helps others inspect the
workﬂow and test generated families under their own assumptions.

D Supplementary Mathematical Preliminaries
D.1 Geometric intuition for valid inequalities
Let conv(S) denote the convex hull of S, i.e., the smallest convex set that contains S. Valid inequalities
tighten P toward conv(S), narrowing the gap between the LP optimum and the true MILP optimum. Adding
inequalities that are valid for conv(S) can separate some fractional extreme points of P and can reduce the
integrality gap. Optimality-preserving inequalities need not be valid for conv(S) but can still speed up branch-
and-bound by pruning provably suboptimal regions. Fig. 6 provides a schematic view of the integer feasible
set S, its LP relaxation P , and the convex hull conv(S). It also contrasts (i) valid inequalities that remove
fractional LP optima while preserving all points in conv(S), and (ii) optimality-preserving inequalities that
may remove some feasible points but keep the optimal objective value unchanged. SAGE instead generates
acceleration inequality families that are empirically checked on an admissibility set (Section 4).

D.2 Polyhedra and Facets
A polyhedron is any set Q in Rn that can be described by ﬁnitely many linear inequalities. The constraints
Ax≤ b are the linear inequalities that deﬁne Q.
Q = { x∈ Rn : Ax≤ b}.
Polyhedra are convex, and if Q is bounded, it is called a polytope.
A face of a polyhedron Q is a set of the form F := Q∩{ x∈ Rn : cx = β} where cx≤ β is a valid inequality
for Q. We say that the valid inequality cx≤ β deﬁnes the face F of the polyhedron Q. The equation
cx = β deﬁnes a supporting hyperplane that intersects Q. A facet is a face of maximum dimension (i.e., one
dimension less than that of the polyhedron).
For example, consider a cube as a 3D polytope in R3 and a plane as the supporting hyperplane. Intersecting
a cube with a supporting hyperplane may result in a 0D corner point, a 1D straight line, or a 2D plane. The
corner point, the line (edge of a cube), and the 2D plane are all faces of the 3D cube. However, only the 2D
plane is a facet of the 3D cube.
D.3 Facet-Deﬁning Valid Inequalities
In integer programming, valid inequalities that are facet-deﬁning for conv(S) are especially important because
they are necessary and suﬃcient for characterizing conv(S). Once conv(S) is characterized, solving the MILP
over S reduces to the linear program of maximizing c⊤x + h⊤y over conv(S), which is polynomially solvable.

The ﬁrst inequality prevents any active strip from crossing the row’s unique hole. The second inequality
counts row components: if the hole is in an interior column, the covered cells split into two disconnected
intervals. Otherwise they form one interval. The third inequality follows from column uniqueness: the
column that was a hole in row i−1 must be covered in row i, so at least one rectangle starts in every row
after the ﬁrst. The fourth tightens the vacated-column inequality to require a start covering the exact column
vacated by the previous row’s hole. The ﬁnal two inequalities handle intervals broken by the new hole: if
the new hole at k breaks an interval from row i−1, the new start covering j cannot cross k. Together with
the top-ﬂow equality, these inequalities give the LP relaxation the objective lower bound ∑ i,(a,b) sab i ≥ N .

F.7 Sub-Hour Unit Commitment (SHUC)
The SHUC benchmark is a unit commitment problem with a horizon under one hour that decides generator
commitment (on/oﬀ) and dispatch levels to meet demand and reserve. SHUC is a problem motivated by
practice with many constraints. Some benchmark instances used in our suite are included in MIPLIB Koch
et al. (2011). We use the PGLib-UC v19.08 JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) benchmark Power Grid Lib
(2019) and follow its reference formulation with piecewise production costs, startup categories, ramping limits,
and minimum up and down times Knueven et al. (2020). For source attribution, we use the recommended
documents for the ca cases ( Knueven et al. , 2020), the ferc cases ( Knueven et al. , 2020; Krall et al. , 2012),
and the rts_gmlc cases ( Barrows et al. , 2019).
